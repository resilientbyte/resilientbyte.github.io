What the dinosaurs know

Seventeen years in enterprise software, one comet on the horizon, and a question I can no longer ignore.

On this page

  1. 01Down in the basement
  2. 02What history says about comets
  3. 03My working hypothesis

It’s 2026. AI agents are drafting contracts, writing code and answering customer emails. Meanwhile, a large share of the world’s biggest companies is still in the middle of migrating from SAP ECC to S/4HANA, a product launched more than ten years ago.

I’ve spent my whole career on the slow side of that contrast. I’m an SAP guy. And for a while now, one question has been on my mind: is everything I know about to become worthless?

This blog is my attempt to answer that question honestly. I’m writing it in public because I suspect I’m not the only one asking.

Down in the basement

I graduated in business informatics in 2009 and joined a small IT consultancy near Munich that implemented CRM systems across Europe. The dominant vendor was SAP. My only prior contact with the company from Walldorf had been a university FI/CO course, which consisted of typing values from a script into SAP GUI fields. If you got one wrong, you were toast. Well, SAP hasn’t become much more forgiving since.

Over the years, I worked my way deeper into the SAP world. It wasn’t always exciting work, and it certainly wasn’t always meaningful. But I found myself increasingly drawn to the big systems at the heart of companies like BMW, Siemens and Airbus: the ones that quietly make sure parts get ordered, invoices get paid and cars roll off the line.

In 2016, I joined my first S/4HANA project, and that marked the start of a new chapter. For the past ten years, much of my work has revolved around moving companies from their old ERP landscapes to SAP’s new generation. It’s slow, demanding work, and it has taught me how much more than software is at stake in these projects: decades of business processes, custom code and organisational habits all have to move along with the data. It also explains why so many companies have yet to finish that journey.

SAP doesn’t have much of a fan club among its end users. Anyone who has ever created a sales order through a maze of input screens that look straight out of the 1990s knows why. Yet it runs a remarkable share of the world’s business, and it does so stably, reliably and without much fuss. SAP experts have always been a bit like the basement kids of the IT business. But if the people in the basement stopped working, the lights upstairs would go out pretty quickly.

What history says about comets

One of my specialisations at university was innovation and technology management. The pattern I learned there still holds: every fundamental breakthrough splits people into prophets and doomsayers, and reality usually disappoints both.

When cloud computing took off, many predicted the end of the system administrator. Why would anyone need people to run servers when Amazon could do it for them? Instead, the knowledge moved. Many of those admins became the cloud architects and DevOps engineers who design today’s infrastructure. The servers disappeared into the cloud. The expertise didn’t. It changed shape.

So let me apply that lens to my own fear. Yes, AI looks like the comet that ends the age of the ERP dinosaurs. But look at what agentic AI actually needs to be useful in a large enterprise: clean master data, well-defined processes, reliable integrations, authorisation concepts, audit trails, and someone who understands why the purchase order workflow at a car manufacturer has a dozen approval steps. That’s not the opposite of ERP knowledge. That is ERP knowledge.

My working hypothesis

Here is what I currently believe, and what I want to test on this blog:

The knowledge locked inside enterprise core systems is about to become more valuable, not less, but only for people willing to carry it into a new form.

The monoliths will change. Some will shrink into data and transaction backbones, others will dissolve into services that AI agents orchestrate. The people who understand both sides, how the business really runs and what the new technology can do, will be the ones designing what comes next.

Over the coming months, I want to explore questions like:

  • Will tomorrow’s enterprise still have an ERP at its core, or something entirely different?
  • Is master data quality the real make-or-break factor for AI in ERP?
  • Which SAP skills will still matter in five years, and which ones are really dinosaur bones?

I don’t have the answers yet. That’s the point. If you work anywhere near enterprise software and you’re asking yourself the same things, follow along here or on LinkedIn, and tell me where you think I’m wrong.
