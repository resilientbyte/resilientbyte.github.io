Lost in the blueprint

Thousands of pages, dozens of experts, and still no picture of how the business really works.

  2. 02Solutions in disguise

A scene many of you will know: you need to onboard yourself onto a new project, and the first thing you get is a link to a SharePoint site where documents have been pouring in for years. Along with the reassuring promise that everything you need is in there.

This time, everything really was in there. Hundreds of folders, thousands of documents. Presentation slides, PDF libraries, process diagrams and session recordings. I spent days trying to dive into this lake of information, without ever getting my feet wet.

Every single page made sense on its own. Yet after all that reading, I could not have told you how the business actually worked. Where the money came from. Where things went wrong. What kept people awake at night.

My first explanation was obvious: too many documents, badly maintained, half of them outdated. A classic documentation problem.

It was wrong.

Two hours with a colleague

A few weeks ago, I sat down with a colleague who has been working in this area for more than 20 years. I asked her about a specific business scenario, expecting a few pointers to the right documents.

Instead, we talked it through. I asked a lot of questions, many of them naive, and every answer raised the next one. Step by step, a picture formed in my head. Processes I had only known as separate documents began to connect, until the whole thing came together.

It took roughly two hours. Afterwards, I understood more than after three days in the archive.

She did not walk me through systems or transactions. She started with the problem the business was trying to solve, and everything else fell into place around it. She gave me less information than the archive, but in the right order.

And the real value was not a transfer of information from her head to mine. It was the dialogue. The documents answered questions I had never asked. She answered the ones I actually had, and helped me find the ones I should have asked.

That was when I realised the documents were not the problem. The way I had been reading them was.

Solutions in disguise

I read the archive the way I have read documents for more than 15 years: as an IT person.

I was looking for requirements. Interfaces. Data objects. Customizing. Every paragraph was quietly translated into one question: what does the system need to do? And while I was still reading, I was already designing. I had solutions before I understood the problem.

In the SAP world, we even built a method around this habit. The Business Blueprint was the phase in classic SAP projects where requirements were collected from the business and written down, often in hundreds of pages. Collect, document, sign off, build.

The trouble is that most requirements are solutions in disguise. Take a typical one:

“We need a report of open receivables by due date.”

It sounds like a requirement. In fact, it is already a solution. The real problem behind it might be: “We notice liquidity shortfalls too late.” That sentence appears nowhere. I have spent years in IT projects trying to solve problems the business never actually put into words.

A thousand pages of requirements give you a thousand fragments of solutions. They do not give you a picture of what the business is trying to achieve.

My colleague could do something I had never consciously practised: frame a problem so that it becomes solvable. That is often harder than the solution itself.

Until now

For a long time, this weakness did not matter much. Somebody else did the business side. I did the solution. That division of labour has paid my bills for my entire career. Until now.

Look at how I have actually spent my time: writing specifications, designing customizing and enhancements, drafting technical concepts and interface mappings, documenting what was built.

Now ask, for each of these: how much can AI already do today, and how much in two years? Not all of it, not yet. But the direction is clear. Each of these tasks is getting cheaper and faster, and the quality improves with every model generation.

When solutions become cheap, the bottleneck moves. The scarce skill is no longer building the solution. It is knowing whether you are solving the right problem.

My advantage was always in the how. The how is losing its value.

From tool to value

So what do I change? Two things: how I approach a problem, and what I am willing to accept as a solution.

First, the problem before the tool. It starts with the first question I ask. Not “What should the system do?” but “What goes wrong today, and what does it cost?” Systems and tools stay out of the conversation until the answer fits into one sentence. Behind every requirement, I keep asking “what for?” until I reach a business goal. And I treat conversations like the one with my colleague as the primary source, and documents as secondary.

Second, openness. Not every problem needs software, and not every piece of software belongs in the core system. Sometimes the answer is a changed process, a data clean-up, an AI agent, or simply leaving something out.

SAP itself has walked a similar path. Its current methodology, SAP Activate, has replaced the classic Business Blueprint with fit-to-standard workshops. They start from the standard and ask only where the business really differs, and why. At its core, that is a method for framing the problem.

Putting it to the test

Good intentions are cheap. So I am testing this on the least promising case I can think of.

I am currently preparing a technical migration in an SAP landscape. It is necessary, not optional. Migrations are usually framed the same way: a list of technical measures, a budget, a risk register. The business sees a cost block and a disruption.

I want to ask a different question. Not: What do we technically have to do to migrate? But: Since we have to touch everything anyway, which business problem can we solve along the way?

For a single item on the list, that looks like this:

  • The usual way: “Upgrade custom development X to the new release.”
  • The reframed way: “Does the problem X once solved still exist? And if so, does the standard solve it better today?”

Every custom development, every workaround, every special case becomes a question about the business, not about the system.

Will it work? I honestly do not know yet. I will report back.

Out of the trap

We often talk about vendor lock-in. But there is a second kind that nobody puts in a contract: the one in our heads. If you only think in one system, you can only solve problems inside that system.

That does not mean throwing away what I know. More than 15 years of ERP experience are not a burden but a foundation. Understanding how processes, data and money flow through an enterprise is exactly the kind of deep knowledge that will not go out of date.

What I want to add is breadth: the ability to understand a business problem and solve it with whatever tool fits. Deep in one field, broad in problem solving. The required knowledge profile would be more T-shaped than siloed.

In my last article, I argued that as AI takes over the craft, the understanding behind it becomes more valuable. This article adds one piece: the most valuable understanding is not of the system, but of the problem it is meant to solve.

The Business Blueprint asked what the business wants to have. The better question is what it wants to achieve.

You don’t get lost in the blueprint if you know first what the house is supposed to do.

Next time: My colleague did not know more than the documents. She had understood them. That is the kind of knowledge I want to build: a deep foundation that lasts, and the ability to pick up everything else on demand. In the next article, I will look at how I need to change the way I learn to get there, while new information keeps flooding in.
