My own brownfield migration into the AI age

For ten years, I've helped companies move from ECC to S/4HANA. Now it's my turn.

On this page

  1. 01Reading what experts already wrote
  2. 02Where the AI runs out of answers
  3. 03From doing to designing
  4. 04The uncomfortable part
  5. 05Back to the hypothesis

Recently, I was asked how we could better document the authorisation roles in our SAP ERP environment. On a whim, I uploaded one of SAP’s standard roles into Copilot and asked it to write a detailed role description in Markdown.

The result was astonishing. Almost every transaction and Fiori app in the role came with an explanation of what it was for and why it belonged there. Before long, I had sketched an entire knowledge management framework around the idea, but that’s a story for another article.

And then an uncomfortable thought crept in. If AI can already explain what an existing role does, how long until it can build new ones that fit neatly into a company’s authorisation concept? What if a single prompt replaces months of work that used to require a well-paid expert?

In my first article, I argued that the knowledge locked inside enterprise core systems is about to become more valuable, not less. This was the first time that hypothesis faced a real test, and my gut reaction was not exactly confident.

Authorisations are not the only area where this is happening. AI-generated ABAP code is already part of everyday work in many SAP shops. AI knowledge assistants promise to make the expertise in consultants’ heads available to anyone. And a new wave of startups claims they can run an S/4HANA migration in days rather than years.

So which parts of our expertise is AI actually replacing, and which parts is it simply making visible?

Reading what experts already wrote

Let me be clear: the result impressed me. But it’s worth asking why it was so good.

An SAP standard role is not a random collection of transactions. It is the result of careful design by people who understood how purchasing, finance or warehouse processes actually work, and who decided which activities belong together and which must be kept apart. Copilot wasn’t inventing anything. It was reading what experts had already written and translating it into plain language.

That’s what large language models are remarkably good at: making existing knowledge accessible. But that knowledge has to exist first. An AI can paint a convincing new version of the Mona Lisa. It could never have painted the first one.

The same is true of SAP itself. SAP doesn’t really sell software. It sells best practice, wrapped in software and collected by human experts over more than fifty years.

Where the AI runs out of answers

Now imagine the same experiment with a role that has grown inside a company over fifteen years. The AI will still tell you what’s in it. What it can’t tell you is why the role looks the way it does. Why one plant has a different cut than another. Which audit finding led to a separate role for invoice approval. Whether half of those authorisations should still be there at all.

That knowledge lives in meeting notes nobody kept and in the heads of the people who were in the room. And even where it is documented, someone still has to weigh it up. The business wants flexibility, the auditors want segregation of duties, and IT wants something it can maintain. Finding a compromise everyone can live with is a judgement call, and someone has to stand behind it when the auditors come knocking.

This is also why I’m sceptical that companies will soon vibe-code their own ERP systems and leave SAP behind. In my experience, writing code is almost never the critical part of a software project. The hard part is agreeing on requirements with people who have a business to run and no particular wish to join an IT project. AI makes the easy part faster. It doesn’t make the hard part go away.

From doing to designing

None of this means nothing changes. Much of the tedious work in IT projects, from building and configuring to testing, will become dramatically faster. AI could draft that authorisation role in minutes. But someone still has to decide which company codes and plants it covers, which risky combinations of authorisations are acceptable, and how it fits into the overall concept.

That’s where the work shifts: from doing to designing. Sharpening requirements, designing architectures that stay stable while systems change faster than ever, and managing the change that comes with it. Overflowing backlogs may become a thing of the past, and IT no longer has to be the brake on the business. But the faster we can build, the more it matters that we build the right thing.

The work doesn’t disappear. It moves up a level.

The uncomfortable part

So far, this sounds like good news for anyone willing to move up a level. But there’s a catch I haven’t been able to shake.

Much of what used to be manual work will soon be almost entirely automated. Building roles, configuring systems, writing standard code: the craft behind these tasks is losing its value fast. Yet someone still has to understand what a good authorisation concept looks like and what happens when it’s missing. The craft is becoming obsolete. The understanding isn’t.

The problem is that the two have always been linked. Most of us didn’t learn authorisation concepts from a textbook. We learned them by building roles, chasing down missing authorisations and untangling segregation-of-duties conflicts until the logic finally clicked. The doing was the learning.

For people like me, that knowledge is already there, even if it stings a little to watch skills I spent years sharpening turn into something a machine does in minutes. For people starting their careers today, the problem is much bigger. If AI builds the roles, where will they learn what makes a role good? How do you become an expert reviewer of work you’ve never done yourself?

We need a new way to understand the craft without spending years practising it.

Back to the hypothesis

So where does this leave the hypothesis from my first article? It survived its first real test, but not unchanged. Here is the refined version:

As AI takes over the craft, the understanding behind it becomes rarer, and therefore more valuable. But only for those who learn to judge and design rather than build.

What that means in practice:

  • The dinosaur bones are the craft: building roles by hand, writing standard code, clicking through configuration screens.
  • What survives is the understanding: why processes work the way they do, what the business really needs, and which compromises are acceptable.
  • The new core skill is judgement: deep enough to tell whether an architecture will hold or an AI-generated solution is quietly wrong, but not so deep that you have to build it all yourself.
  • The way to learn it is questioning, not doing: asking the AI not only to build something, but to explain why it’s built that way, what would break if you changed it, and what the alternatives would be.

It’s a bit like pilots training in a simulator: they learn how the aircraft behaves without having to crash one. Our profession needs its own version of that, and it applies to me as much as to anyone starting out. I won’t go back to university or start over as a junior in five new disciplines. In S/4HANA projects, we call that kind of approach brownfield: you keep what has proven its worth and move it onto a new platform instead of starting from scratch. My SAP knowledge remains the foundation. What changes is what I build on top of it.

Not a greenfield restart. A brownfield migration. And like every good migration, it starts with knowing what’s worth keeping.

A note on how this was written: the ideas, experiences and opinions in this article are my own, and so is every decision about what stays and what goes. I use AI as a sparring partner to challenge my drafts and sharpen the wording. That seems only fitting for a blog about humans and AI working together.
